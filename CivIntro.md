#CIVMILAIR TWO
Civmilair Two is an extended and expanded version of Civmilair which was published in October 1984. The intention of compiling Civmilair was to provide an alpha-numeric list of British military serials which had a link with British civil aircraft registrations - to provide a reference for spotters and researchers To further explain, it would be appropriate here to reproduce the introduction to Civmilair.

##CIVMILAIR
Following the cessation of hostilities at the end of World War One, the Royal Air Force (as the Royal Flying Corps was then known) no longer had a pressing need for pilots and, consequently, large numbers of training aircraft became surplus to requirements. The end of wartime operations also meant a reduction in the numbers of bombers and fighters. All of these surplus aircraft were disposed of with the majority being sold in the civil market.
Private flying was virtually unknown before the War but, with the coming of peace, there was an upsurge of interest in flying by "the man in the street". As a consequence, large numbers of flying clubs were formed in this country using surplus military aircraft as their equipment. The aircraft used by most clubs was the Avro 504 and this type was the mainstay of clubs until, some years later, the new designs, such as the Moth, became available.
The civilianised bombers were, in the early 1920s, designed to carry freight or, after modification, a few passengers and these aircraft formed the fleet of airliners used in this country until demand for air travel grew to such an extent that new types of airliners had to be built to cope with the expansion.
A relatively few ex RAF fighters were civilianised and these were not used generally for private flying but for more specialised tasks such as the SE.5A used for sky-writing and racing purposes.
Apart from the large numbers of military aircraft transferred to the British civil aircraft register, some civil aircraft assumed military identities when used by the Forces for evaluation purposes.
Similarly, after World War Two, many surplus aircraft were sold in the civil market and were registered as British civil aircraft. Large numbers of Rapides, Ansons and Dakotas were snapped up by small and medium-sized independent airlines and  charter operators. The York and Halifax are further examples of former military aircraft which received intensive use from civil operators for long distance and heavy transport work with many finding themselves on the Berlin airlift. Several commercial types were given "trooping" serials between 1953 and 1956 for operations into the Suez Canal zone where only  military aircraft were permitted. Serials were mainly in the WW and XF blocks and the main types used were Hermes, Yorks, Vikings, Dakotas and a few Bristol Freighters.
Since 1945, the military aircraft fleets have continued to re-equip with more modern types and, again primarily with training aircraft, large numbers of surplus machines have been civilianised. The Hawk Trainer (known as the Magister in RAF use) and the Tiger Moth were the aircraft which formed the bulk of this transfer with the Tiger Moth being the type arriving in greatest numbers onto the civil register. In the early post-war years, it would have been difficult to have found a flying club not equipped with Hawk Trainers, Tiger Moths or Austers, although most of the latter type would have had no military service having been built for the civil market.
The disposal of surplus military aircraft has continued with, in more recent times, the Chipmunk, Beagle Basset and Sioux, finding private and corporate owners in this country.
The traffic is not all one way for, as mentioned earlier, some civil aircraft were taken on by the military and given military serials. The greatest number of such transfers occurred during World war Two when private and flying club aircraft were impressed into military service for training, communication and light transport use. Most of these aircraft were camouflaged and carried military serials but some, which were found to be unsuitable for military flying, were used for ground instruction and as decoys at dummy airfields. After the War, most of the surviving aircraft were restored to the civil register with some acquiring a new registration in the process.
More recently, the VC.10s and TriStars of British Airways have been acquired by the Ministry of Defence for use as tankers and transports respectively.
Since 1920, the British civil aircraft registration has been prefixed with the nationality identification letter "G". This familiar form of registration comprises the permanent register. However, prevoiusly, a dual system of registration was used. All military aircraft sold into the civil market were operated with the serial as the official registration but with all other military markings (roundels, squadron markings and the like) obliterated. New aircraft built for the civil market, or from spares, or with no previous military service were registered in a special series commencing K-100. This latter system was a temporary measure used for a relatively short period. The last allocation in the temporary system was K-175 to an Avro 536. This registration was not taken up and the last K series registration believed to have been used was K-169, a Sopwith Gnu.
With the advent of the permanent registration system, civil aircraft flying with military serials and those in the K series were re-registered into the permanent G- system. So far as can be ascertained, although the K series was not intended for former military machines, two ex-military aircraft did acquire such markings, but only after a conversion of type. The Airco DH.9 serialled H9277 was converted to the prototype DH.9B and registered K-109; it subsequently became G-EAAC. K-162 was carried by a Handley Page 0/7 which had been converted from the 0/400 C9704; it later became G-EAGN.
From the early days to the present UK military serials have been issued broadly in a logical numeric and later alphanumeric system. First allocations began at 1 until 10000 was reached after which the serial block A1 to Z9999 was used. This was then followed by AA100 and has progressed until ZZ999 has nearly been reached. Early Naval aircraft were, however, serialled in the block N1 to N9999 part of which, N1000 to N9999, was reissued when the standard system reached that block. The mid inter-war period was fairly static with serials from J6500 onwards until the massive build-up for war production commenced around L1000; AZ999 was allocated in 1940. Interspersed with these were numerous civil aircraft which were requisitioned or "impressed", mainly for communication or training roles. Most of these impressed aircraft were in the W,X,Z,AV,AW,BB and BD blocks with MA serials being used for aircraft used in India. The mass of "lend-lease" production were contained between EA100 nand HD999. Many serials were issued out of phase as orders for tens of thousands of aircraft were placed with hundreds of manufacturers and sub-contractors. Intentional "black-out" or non-allocated serials and cancelled orders resulted in many gaps in those blocks. Th post-war period was heralded around VP100. Static airframes used for training purposes are usually given "Maintenance" serials with an M suffix i.e. 1234M whilst latterly the Royal Navy has used serials with an A prefix i.e. A1234.
A study of the British Civil Aircraft Register will reveal that well over 5000 aircraft on the register were once or subsequently became British military aircraft. The civil register is, of course, compiled alphabetically in the order in which the registrations were allotted (apart from the case of the "personalised" registrations which are now so common). The result is  that there is no easy way of determining with which civil registration any particular military serial might be connected. To provide such a reference, Civmilair has been produced with all the previous or subsequent military identities of British civil aircraft being listed in strict order - alphabetically and numerically. It is hoped Civmilair will prove to be a convenience and of interest to researchers of the British civil and military registers.

##NOTES
I have listed (hopefully) all military serials which have a connection with a British civil aircraft registration and have included spurious markings since, today, some civil aircraft are flying with such bogus military serials.
Since Civmilair is ideally used in conjunction with the British Civil Aircraft Register, I have not indicated whether the military serial was the previous or subsequent serial of the aircraft concerned - this information can be obtained from the civil register. For the same reason, I have given no details of any foreign registrations which may have been borne by the aircraft at some time during the transfer between the British civil and military registers.

###Serial
lists the military serial in alphanumeric order. Some serials appear more than once - usually because the aircraft in its civil guise has been re-registered and thus provides more than one connection between the two registers. A serial followed by (1) or (2) denotes that that is the first or second allocation of the serial. Spurious serials are enclosed in qotation marks " " and those serials which were not taken up are enclosed in brackets ( ).
###Type
aircraft type (and mark where applicable). It is appropriate to mention the Airspeed Oxford here. It was originally designated the AS.10 and did not become the AS.40 until civilianised. The original designation has been retained.
###Reg'n
shows the civil registration allocated to, and generally carried by, the aeroplane. As with "serial", a number in brackets after the registration indicates the registration has been allocated to more than one aircraft.
###Notes
The notes are generally self-explanatory. Details included are re-registrations, the retyping of aircraft due to conversion (e.g. from Auster 6 to Terrier, Tiger Moth to Jackaroo, Oxford to Consul), the previous serials of aircraft which have carried more than one serial and an indication of aircraft which have acquired civil registrations solely for ferry purposes to foreign air forces. (ntu = not taken up).

The information contained in this publication has been gleaned from many other publications generally available over the last 25 years or so and my main sorces have been:-

Aeroplane  Monthly, Air Pictorial, Aviation News and Review (LAAS), British Civil Aircraft 1919-1959 (A.J.Jackson), British Civil Aircraft Register (LAAS), British Civil Aircraft Registers 1919-1978 (Midland Counties Publications), The British Civil Aircraft Register 1919-28 and 1928-30 (Air Britain), British Military Aircraft Serials and Markings (BARG), British Military Aircraft Serials (Bruce Robertson), DC.3 Production (LAAS), de Havilland Aircraft since 1915 (A.J.Jackson), Flypast, Foreign Registers (LAAS and sundry) and Impressments Log (Air Britain).
